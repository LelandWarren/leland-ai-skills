# least-regret

Judges a fork from six months out. The promise is a clearer pick that fits their values; never promise less regret. Imagined regret runs hot, because people overpredict how long a sting lasts, so the number you compare is the sting **left** on the look-back date, never the peak.

Run it in one pass. Put your best read in every step and mark each number the user should overrule as *your read*. They correct; you re-run only the steps their correction moves.

## Steps

### 1. Frame the fork

Name the decision in one line and list 2–3 options. One option is always **do nothing** (or "decide later", when waiting is real). You may add one option they didn't name, such as a trial, a smaller version, or a delay with a date, when it plausibly beats theirs; mark it *added*. If the decision itself is missing from the conversation, ask one question to get it and wait.

Done when the fork has a one-line name and every option, do-nothing included.

### 2. Door check

Ask: can the user try this and reverse it within a week, with only small, bounded friction for the people involved? Friction during the try counts as small when it ends the moment they reverse.

- **Two-way door**: show the fork, then the pick that is fastest to try and learn from, one line on why, and the signal that would make them reverse it. Skip steps 3–6, and offer the full run in one line.
- **One-way door**: go on to step 3.

Done when the door type is stated with the one fact that decides it.

### 3. Six-month look

Set the look-back date to today plus six months, as a real date. For each option, standing on that date:

- **Story**: what likely happened, in one or two lines.
- **Peak**: how much it hurts at its worst, 1–10.
- **Left**: how much sting remains on the look-back date, 1–10. Ask what they would have adapted to, explained, or fixed by then.
- **Who else pays**: the people who carry this option's cost (their team, a peer, a customer).

Done when every option has all four lines and you compare options on **Left**.

### 4. Horizon check

Name which horizon their gut is serving: this week's relief or dread, or the look-back date. If they stated a lean, use it. If they didn't, read it from the pressure in their words (a deadline, who is waiting, what they keep returning to) and mark it *your read*. If the gut pick and the lowest-**Left** pick differ, name the gap plainly.

Done when the favored horizon is named and any gap is stated.

### 5. Pick and pre-mortem

Commit to the option with the lowest **Left**, after weighing who else pays. Break a tie toward the more reversible option, then toward the one that costs others less. One sentence, no hedge.

Then the **pre-mortem**: it is the look-back date and the pick went badly. Write the three likeliest reasons, one line each. For each, name a guard they can add now or mark it an accepted risk. If a reason is strong enough to flip the pick, flip it and say so, including the condition that would trigger the flip.

Done when the pick is one sentence and each of the three reasons has a guard or an accepted-risk note.

### 6. Write the prediction

Give them a three-line record they can keep:

```
Pick: <option> — decided <today>
Confidence the pick is right: <1–10>
Early signal by <date ~6 weeks out>: <what they'd see if it was right>
```

Offer two follow-ups in one line each, and do them only on their yes: save the record as a memory, and create a calendar reminder on the look-back date to compare the outcome with this prediction.

Done when the record is shown and both offers are made.

## Answer shape

Lead with the pick (or the two-way-door pick) in the first line. Then the steps in order, each under a short bold label, with the six-month look as a small table: option, story, peak, left, who else pays. Keep a two-way-door answer under 15 lines and a full run under 45.
